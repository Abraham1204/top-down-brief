## 1. Why the pyramid works

- Readers can hold only about seven items in short-term memory, so they group and summarize what they read. If the writer does not supply the grouping, the reader has to invent it, and often gets it wrong or gives up.
- Readers take sentences in one at a time. Making them read ahead and back to see how ideas connect is bad manners toward the reader.
- Top-down order (main idea first, then the ideas it summarizes) lets the reader know what to look for before the details arrive.
- Every document supports **one single thought**: the one that sums up the final set of groupings. If there are two, there are two documents, or a higher thought is missing.

## 2. The vertical relationship

- Every box in the pyramid is an **idea**: a statement that tells the reader something they do not already know, and so raises a question in their mind ("Why?", "How?", "How do you know?", "Which?").
- The level below answers that question, and only that question.
- Keep raising and answering questions until the reader would have no further logical questions. Stop there; do not add material that answers questions nobody is asking.
- Test: read any point, ask "the reader will now ask...?", and check that the points directly beneath it answer exactly that.

## 3. The horizontal relationship

Points grouped under one idea must relate to one another either deductively or inductively, never both.

**Deductive** (a chain of reasoning)
1. A statement about a situation in the world.
2. A related statement about the subject or predicate of the first.
3. The implication of both being true together ("therefore...").
- Summarize a deductive group by leading with its conclusion: "Because X, we should Y."
- Deduction is heavy at the key line: the reader must hold every step until the "therefore". Use it at the key line only when the reader needs the reasoning before they will accept the action. Push it lower in the pyramid wherever possible.
- Chain no more than four steps.

**Inductive** (a set of like ideas)
- Several ideas of the same kind that together support an inference ("French, German and Russian tanks are at the Polish border" → "Poland is about to be invaded").
- The ideas must be describable by **one plural noun** (reasons, problems, steps, changes, risks, benefits). If they cannot be, the grouping is wrong.
- Induction is easier to read and should be the default at the key line. Present actions before the argument for them, unless the action is unexpected.

## 4. The three rules of any grouping

1. **Summarize**: the idea at any level is a summary of the ideas grouped beneath it.
2. **Same kind**: the ideas in a grouping are the same kind of idea (the plural-noun test).
3. **Logical order**: there is a specific reason why the second idea comes second and cannot come first or third.

## 5. Logical order

Every grouping follows one of three orders, and the order reveals the analysis behind it:

- **Time order** (cause and effect, steps in a process): used when the points are actions taken to reach an effect. First step first. All steps must be at the same level of detail.
- **Structural order** (dividing a whole into parts): used when the points describe the parts of something (regions, business units, components). The division must be **MECE**: mutually exclusive (no overlaps) and collectively exhaustive (nothing missing). Order them as they are naturally seen or as the process flows.
- **Degree order** (ranking like things): used when the points share a characteristic, such as a set of problems or reasons. Put the most important first.

If you cannot tell which order a grouping follows, the thinking behind it is not finished.

## 6. Avoiding intellectually blank assertions

A summary that only announces a category ("There are three problems with the process", "We considered several factors", "The following issues were identified") is intellectually blank. It gives the reader nothing to understand and usually hides unfinished thinking.

Fix it by stating what the grouped points *mean*:
- For **action** groupings, state the effect of taking the actions together: "Three changes will cut processing time from 10 days to 4."
- For **situation** groupings, state what the points have in common and why it matters: "All three delays trace back to manual approvals in finance."

To find the insight: check the group is MECE, look for the similarity in subjects or predicates across the points, then ask "so what?" of that similarity.

## 7. Stating actions as end results

Most business writing is about actions. Vague action statements ("improve coordination", "strengthen the process", "look into options") cannot be checked or delivered.

- Word each action as the tangible result it produces: "Improve profit by 10% by 15 January", not "improve profitability".
- Visualize the finished result of each action; if you cannot picture it, the wording is too vague.
- Keep actions at one level: separate the actions *you* take from the steps inside them.
- Make sure the actions in a group together produce the stated effect, with no gaps.
- Distinguish causes from effects: an action and its outcome are not two items in the same list.

## 8. Common failures and fixes

| Failure | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Buried lead | Background and history first; the request appears in paragraph 3 or the last line | Move the ask or conclusion to sentence one; compress history into one S-C line or cut it |
| Topic, not message | Subject "Q3 update"; heading "Budget" | Write the point: "Q3 revenue 8% ahead of plan; hiring still behind" |
| Mixed kinds | Key line mixes a reason, a step and a risk | Sort by kind; move risks under their own point or to a "risks" section |
| Overlapping points | Two points say the same thing in different words | Merge them; re-check MECE |
| Gap | The points do not fully support the conclusion | Add the missing point, or soften the conclusion to what is supported |
| Too many points | 7+ bullets at the same level | Group into 2 to 5 higher-level points, each with a summary |
| Chronology as argument | "First we..., then we..., then we found..." | Put the finding first; history belongs in the introduction or not at all |
| Deduction at the top when not needed | Long reasoning before the recommendation | Recommendation first; reasons after, inductively |
| Blank summary | "There are several issues" | State the insight the issues share (section 6) |
| Vague ask | "Let me know your thoughts" | Specific action + deadline: "Please confirm option B by Thursday 1 Oct" |
| Unordered list | Points in the order they occurred to the writer | Apply time, structure or degree order |
