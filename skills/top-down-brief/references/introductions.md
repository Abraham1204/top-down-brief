# Introductions: Situation, Complication, Question

The introduction tells the reader a story they already know, so they arrive at the question the document answers in the same frame of mind as the writer.

- **Situation**: a non-controversial statement about the subject that the reader will accept as true. Establishes time and place.
- **Complication**: something that happened, or could happen, in that situation that creates tension. It is what triggers the question.
- **Question**: follows from the complication. It is often implied rather than written.
- **Answer**: the governing thought, the top of the pyramid.

Rules:
- Remind, don't inform. Anything the reader needs to learn goes in the body.
- Keep it proportionate: one sentence for a colleague who knows the context; two or three short paragraphs at most for a long report.
- Put history here, not in the body. The body is for ideas and their logic.
- The order can vary to change tone:
  - Standard (S-C-A): neutral, the default.
  - Direct (A-S-C): answer first, then context. Best for email and busy executives.
  - Concerned (C-S-A): leads with the complication. Use when urgency is the point.
  - Question-led (Q-S-C-A): for a question the reader explicitly asked.

To build one: write the subject, decide the reader's question, write the answer, then work back to the Situation and Complication that make that question inevitable. Re-check that the Complication really raises the Question you are answering.

## Patterns for common situations

Each pattern lists the S-C-Q and the usual key line.

### 1. Giving direction (directive, instruction to a team)
- **S**: We are doing X / you are responsible for Y.
- **C**: To do it, we need Z from you.
- **Q**: What should I do? (How?)
- **Key line**: the steps, in time order, each worded as a result. End with the deadline and who to contact.

### 2. Seeking approval to spend money
- **S**: We have a problem / opportunity (known to the reader).
- **C**: Solving it requires spending $N.
- **Q**: Should we spend it?
- **Answer**: Yes; please approve $N for X by [date].
- **Key line**: the reasons (benefit, cost justified, risk of not acting), in degree order. Include the cost, the payback and the exact approval requested.

### 3. Explaining how to do something
- **S**: We need to achieve X.
- **C**: It is not obvious how / the current way isn't working.
- **Q**: How should we do it?
- **Key line**: the steps, in time order, at one level of detail.

### 4. Choosing among alternatives
- **S**: We need to achieve X (state the goal clearly).
- **C**: There are several ways to do it.
- **Q**: Which is best?
- **Answer**: Option B.
- **Key line**: why B meets the goal better than the others, on the criteria that matter. Present alternatives only if the reader already knows about them or asked; otherwise put the comparison in an appendix table. Many "which option?" questions disappear once the desired result is defined precisely.

### 5. Recommending a change to a process
- **Reader knows the current process**: S = how it works now; C = what is wrong with it (cost, delay, error); Q = how should we change it? Key line = the changes, in the order they would be implemented, each with its effect.
- **Reader does not know the current process**: give a brief before/after picture first, then the S-C-Q above.

### 6. Proposal (to a client or internal sponsor)
- **S**: The reader's goal or situation.
- **C**: The problem or obstacle to it.
- **Q**: How can it be solved / why should we do it with you?
- **Key line**, choose one:
  - *Steps structure*: the approach, step by step, with deliverables.
  - *Reasons structure*: why we are the right choice (useful when competing).
- Qualifications, team, fees and terms are peripheral; put them after the argument.
- For a new reader, spend a little more on the problem to show you understand it; for a known reader, keep it short.

### 7. Progress review / status update
- **S**: We agreed to do X by [date].
- **C**: We are at stage N; here is what has changed or what we found.
- **Q**: Where do things stand, and what do you need to do?
- **Answer**: the one-line status ("On track for launch on 14 Nov; one risk needs your decision").
- **Key line**: findings or workstreams, ordered by importance, with decisions needed flagged first.

### 8. Why did it happen? (explanation, incident, variance)
- **S**: What was expected.
- **C**: What happened instead.
- **Q**: Why, and what are we doing about it?
- **Answer**: the root cause and the fix.
- **Key line**: causes in degree order, then the actions and their effects. Separate cause from effect.

### 9. Bad news and sensitive messages
- One sentence of context, then the news plainly, then what it means for the reader, then what happens next and who to contact.
- Do not bury the news after pleasantries. Do not over-apologize or speculate.
- Keep S-C short and factual; empathy lives in the wording, not in padding.

### 10. Responding to a request or question
- Skip S-C: the reader asked. Open with the answer.
- Key line: the reasons or details they need to act on it.
